\chapter{Results and Discussions}
\label{ch:results}

\section{Results}
\label{sec:res-results}

\subsection{Introduction}
\label{sec:res-intro}

This section reports the outcome of the experiments defined in \Cref{ch:model}. It opens with descriptive statistics: the fine-tuning dataset, the training run, the text generated by the pretrained and the fine-tuned model on held-out prompts, the behaviour of the deployed system, and the ratings collected in the phase-1 user study. It then presents the inferential results, in which the two models are compared on the same held-out samples with paired tests. All phase-2 figures are computed on test stories that were not used in training or in choosing the training settings. Results from the two evaluation phases are kept apart: every rating by a human participant belongs to phase 1 and was obtained with a pretrained model.

\subsection{Descriptive Statistics}
\label{sec:res-descriptive}

\subsubsection{Dataset}

The fine-tuning dataset contains 3,695 samples built from 1,000 stories (\Cref{tab:dataset}). Of the 3,321 training samples, 893 are story openings, 1,122 continuations, 501 climaxes and 805 resolutions. The openings cover all four question budgets: 460 use the full set of 17 questions, 223 use 12, 134 use 8 and 76 use 4, which follows the sampling probabilities of \Cref{sec:model-dataset}. A training sample has 721 words on average, of which 264 are the story segment the model has to write. In tokens, the median sample has 971 and the longest 1,285. The test set has 191 samples from 49 stories; one of the 50 test stories was too short to give a sample.

\subsubsection{Training Run}

\Cref{fig:training} shows the loss during fine-tuning. Before training, the loss of the pretrained model on the validation samples was 1.476. It fell to 1.207 after 150 steps, 1.164 after 300, 1.151 after 450 and 1.107 after 600 steps (\Cref{tab:valloss}). The training loss, averaged over 20 steps, moved from 1.46 at the start to between 1.09 and 1.32 in the second half of the run. Most of the gain came in the first 150 steps, which is expected: the model first learns the form of the answer, that is, to begin directly with story text of the requested kind and length, and then improves more slowly. The validation loss was still falling when the run ended, so the model had not begun to overfit. The run processed 192,000 story tokens in about one hour.

\subsubsection{Generated Text and Speed}

\Cref{tab:gen-desc} describes the text that the two models generated for the 69 held-out prompts, under the application's own generation settings.

The pretrained model wrote openings of 357 words on average, inside the requested range of 300 to 400 words, and continuations of 312 words. The fine-tuned model wrote shorter segments: 281 words for openings and 260 for continuations, and 33 of its 49 openings were below 300 words. Lexical variety was the same for both models, with about 95\% of word pairs distinct. Neither model reached the limit of 1,000 new tokens in any generation.

Both models generated openings at 38.1 tokens per second. The adapter adds very little computation, so fine-tuning did not slow the model down. Because its segments are shorter, the fine-tuned model finished a segment in 10.2\,s on average against 12.9\,s for the pretrained model, and no segment from either model took longer than 15\,s.

\subsubsection{Deployed System}

In the application, a turn consists of the story segment and a second request for the four choices. In an end-to-end session with the fine-tuned model, run through the REST interface with 17 answers and one user-added question, the opening and its choices took 28\,s and the continuation and its choices 22\,s. The user-added question, which asked for a silver fox as the protagonist's companion, was honoured in the opening, the continuation followed the choice that had been selected, and the choices were returned as four separate options in both turns. \Cref{fig:ui-story} shows an opening written by the fine-tuned model in the interface, with the choices offered for the next step. Switching between the fine-tuned and the pretrained model from the settings panel takes effect without restarting the server.

\subsubsection{Phase-1 User Study}

The ratings in this part were collected in phase 1, with the pretrained Llama 3.1 8B model (\Cref{sec:model-setup}). They describe the framework: the question protocol, the story-flow model and the multimodal features.

Across the 30 stories of the user study, the mean Likert scores were 4.05 for coherence, 4.12 for creativity, 4.35 for engagement and 3.75 for consistency. \Cref{fig:quality} shows the scores by genre. Adventure received the highest engagement score (4.6). Mystery received the lowest consistency score (3.5), which is expected because a mystery depends on clues that must stay consistent across many segments.

\Cref{tab:heldout} reports the loss and perplexity of the pretrained and the fine-tuned model on the held-out test set, and \Cref{fig:heldout} shows the loss by prompt type. Over all 191 samples, fine-tuning reduced the loss from 1.521 to 1.194 and the perplexity from 4.58 to 3.30, a reduction of 28\%. The loss fell for every prompt type. The opening is the hardest prompt for both models, because the model has the least text to build on, and the resolution is the easiest, because most of the story is already fixed.

Because both models were scored on the same samples, the differences can be tested directly (\Cref{tab:paired}). By sample, the mean loss fell by 0.336 (95\% bootstrap confidence interval 0.327 to 0.347), and the fine-tuned model had the lower loss on every one of the 191 samples. Samples from one story are not independent, so the test was repeated on the 49 stories after averaging: the mean loss fell by 0.341 (0.326 to 0.359), and the fine-tuned model was better on all 49 stories. Both the paired $t$-test and the Wilcoxon signed-rank test reject the hypothesis of no difference at both levels ($p < 0.001$ by story), and the effect size is very large (Cohen's $d = 5.75$ by story), because the improvement is nearly the same for every story. \Cref{fig:scatter} plots the two losses for every sample: every point lies below the diagonal, and the improvement is spread over all four prompt types and over easy and hard samples alike.

\subsubsection{Prompt Adherence of Generated Text}

\Cref{tab:adherence} compares the generated text of the two models on the measures of \Cref{sec:model-analysis}, and \Cref{fig:generation} shows the distribution of lengths and the adherence rates.

The clearest difference is in the form of the answer. The pretrained model returned story text alone for 75\% of the prompts. For the others it added something that the application has to remove: in 16 cases a title or heading before the story, usually in bold type, and in one case the line ``To be continued'' after it. The fine-tuned model returned story text alone for all 69 prompts, and the difference is significant (McNemar's exact test, $p < 0.001$).

The second difference is length, and here the pretrained model is better. All of its outputs were within 25\% of the requested range, against 90\% for the fine-tuned model ($p = 0.016$), and the fine-tuned segments were shorter by 69 words on average (Wilcoxon signed-rank test, $p < 0.001$).

The fine-tuned model used the protagonist's name in 88\% of the 60 prompts that give one, against 97\% for the pretrained model. This difference is not statistically significant ($p = 0.12$). In five of the seven fine-tuned outputs without the name, the name in the prompt was not a personal name at all but a word such as ``Defenders'' or ``Guardian'' that the keyword rules had picked from the test story by mistake. There was no difference between the models in complete endings ($p = 1.00$) or in lexical variety ($p = 0.84$).

\subsection{Summary of Findings}
\label{sec:res-summary}

The main findings are as follows.

\begin{enumerate}
    \item Fine-tuning reduced the held-out loss from 1.521 to 1.194 and the perplexity from 4.58 to 3.30 (28\% lower). The loss fell for all four prompt types, for all 191 samples and for all 49 stories, and the difference is statistically significant.
    \item Validation loss fell at every checkpoint, from 1.476 to 1.107, and was still falling after 600 steps.
    \item The fine-tuned model always answered with story text alone (100\% against 75\%), with the same lexical variety and the same generation speed as the pretrained model.
    \item The fine-tuned model wrote shorter segments than requested (275 words on average against 344), and it did not use the protagonist's name more reliably than the pretrained model.
    \item On an 8\,GB laptop the fine-tuned model writes a segment in about 10\,s, and a full turn with choices took 22 to 28\,s.
    \item In the phase-1 user study, with a pretrained model, the framework was rated 4.05 for coherence, 4.12 for creativity, 4.35 for engagement and 3.75 for consistency, with an overall satisfaction of 4.3 out of 5; personalisation grew with the number of questions.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:res-discussion}

\subsection{Introduction}

This section interprets the results, compares them with earlier work, and considers what they mean in practice, where the study is limited and what should be done next.

\subsection{Interpretation of Results}

The held-out comparison answers the central question of phase 2: a short low-rank adaptation on prompts in the system's own format makes the model fit this task better. The improvement is not confined to a few easy cases. It appears for every prompt type and for every test story, and it is large relative to the variation between samples. The model was trained on only 600 samples drawn from the 3,321 available and the validation loss was still falling, so the result shows what a small amount of training achieves, not the limit of the approach.

The generation benchmark shows what the model actually learned, and the picture is mixed. It learned the \emph{form} of the answer completely: it never adds a title or a remark, which the pretrained model does in a quarter of the cases even though the prompt ends with ``Begin the story now''. This matters in practice, because every such addition has to be detected and removed before the text is shown. It also learned the style and the lengths of the training stories, and this is the source of the shorter segments. The training openings have 292 words on average, at the lower edge of the 300 to 400 words that the prompt asks for, and the other segments about 255. The fine-tuned model reproduces these lengths (281 and 260 words) and pays less attention to the number written in the prompt. The cause lies in how the stories were cut into segments, not in the method, and it can be corrected by cutting longer segments.

The use of the protagonist's name points to the second weakness of the data. The answers attached to each training story come from keyword rules, and some of them are wrong; a group or an object is sometimes taken for the protagonist. A model trained on such pairs learns that the name in the prompt is not always the main character, and follows it a little less strictly. The difference from the pretrained model is not significant here, but its direction is a warning that label quality limits how much control fine-tuning can add.

Speed is unchanged because the adapter adds 2.62 million parameters to a model of 3.21 billion. A complete turn, with the request for choices, took under half a minute on a laptop with 8\,GB of memory, with no network connection for the text.

The phase-1 ratings describe the framework and not the fine-tuned model. They show that the structured Q\&A protocol is worth its cost: each additional group of questions raised personalisation, and coherence rose as well, which suggests that the extra information helps the model write a more focused story. The features rated highest were custom answers and illustrations, both of which are cheap to build. The weakest points were the meaningfulness of the offered choices and consistency in mystery stories, which depend on the model and on the simple rule-based checks.

\subsection{Comparison with Previous Research}

Long-form generators such as Re3~\cite{yang2022re3} and DOC~\cite{yang2023doc}, and the planning approach of Farrell and Ware~\cite{farrell2024narrative}, give a story strong structure, but they take their input once and then write on their own. The proposed system gets its structure from the five-phase model and still asks the user for a direction after every chapter. Co-writing tools such as Wordcraft~\cite{yuan2022wordcraft} and Dramatron~\cite{mirowski2023dramatron} give the user more freedom, but the user has to write or edit text; here the user only answers questions and picks directions, and in the phase-1 study custom answers were the most valued feature. Intelligent Grimm~\cite{liu2024grimm} produces better-matched images for a given story, while this system combines a simpler illustration step with narration and music around a generated story.

On the modelling side, the reviewed interactive systems use a general-purpose model without adaptation. The result here agrees with the wider evidence that low-rank adaptation is enough to specialise a large model~\cite{hu2022lora} and that this can be done on quantised weights with modest hardware~\cite{dettmers2023qlora}: 0.082\% of the weights, trained for an hour on a laptop, gave a clear and statistically significant improvement on held-out stories. The work differs from earlier story models trained on prompt-and-story corpora~\cite{fan2018hierarchical} in that the prompts are not free text written by people but the structured prompts of one application, produced by that application's own code.

\subsection{Implications of the Findings}

For developers of language-model applications, the main implication is that the prompt format of an application can be treated as a training target. When the prompts are produced by code, training data in exactly that format can be generated from existing texts without manual annotation, and a short adaptation run then fits the model to the application. The same procedure can be repeated when the prompts change. A second implication is that a hosted model is not required for an interactive product of this kind: a model with three billion parameters, adapted to the task and quantised, writes a story segment in well under half a minute on a laptop, and the user's answers never leave the machine.

For educators, game designers and content creators, the phase-1 results indicate where effort pays off: users valued their own custom answers and the illustrations most, and personalisation grew with the number of questions up to about twelve. For researchers, the code, the dataset builder and the evaluation scripts~\cite{jha2026repo} provide a baseline that can be reproduced on consumer hardware.

\subsection{Limitations of the Study}

The improvement in held-out loss was measured on stories from the same collection as the training data, so it shows that the model learned this kind of story from the system's prompts, not how it behaves on very different writing. The fine-tuned model has not yet been rated by users, because the user study (15 participants) was carried out earlier with a pretrained model. Scene illustrations depend on an online image service, which began to require payment during this work.

\subsection{Recommendations for Future Research}

The work can be taken forward in four directions:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{User study of the fine-tuned model.} Participants should rate stories from the pretrained and the fine-tuned model without knowing which is which.
    \item \textbf{Better training data.} Longer story segments, labels from annotators instead of keyword rules, and training for more steps should correct the short segments and make the model follow every answer more strictly.
    \item \textbf{Local image generation.} A local model such as SDXL~\cite{podell2024sdxl} would remove the dependence on the online image service.
    \item \textbf{Indian languages.} The same dataset and training scripts can be used to fine-tune the model for Hindi and Marathi stories.
\end{itemize}
